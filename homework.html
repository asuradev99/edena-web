1(a–c) · Lattice and the degenerate subspaces

Geometry

For generic V₁ ≠ 0, the primitive translations are a x̂ and a ŷ. Reciprocal vectors are G(nₓ,nᵧ); the first zone is −½ ≤ kₓ/G, kᵧ/G ≤ ½. Γ = (0,0), X = (½,0), M = (½,½).

If V₁ = 0 and V₃ ≠ 0, extra translations (a/2, ±a/2) make a smaller real cell and a larger, diamond-shaped primitive zone. We keep the original square cell as a folding convention. At V₁ = V₃ = 0 there is continuous translation symmetry.

The waves that meet

At X: k and k − (G,0), each at EG/4. At M: k minus (0,0), (G,0), (0,G), or (G,G), each at EG/2.

U±Gx̂ = U±Gŷ = V₁/2. The four diagonal components U(±G,±G) = V₃/2. At M, V₁/2 joins adjacent corners of the four-wave square; V₃/2 joins opposite corners. All other Fourier components vanish.

Plane-wave composition

ψ = Σ cg exp[i(X − g) · r], from the same nine-plane-wave Hamiltonian as the bands. The mean density over one cell is 1. At X, ψ changes sign after translation by a along x, while its density repeats. Global phase changes real and imaginary parts, never density. For a weak positive V₁, the lowest pair approaches sine-like and cosine-like standing waves (up to phase); reversing V₁ swaps them. At degeneracy the eigenbasis is not unique. The finite basis can leave small symmetry errors.

2(a–e) · The two-state solution

Diagonalize first

V₃ has no matrix element inside this pair: their wavevectors differ by (G,0), not a diagonal vector. Remote states contribute beyond this leading resonant approximation.

The direct gap at X is |V₁|, not |V₁|/2. Changing the sign changes the eigenvectors, while these eigenvalues depend only on |V₁|.

Then inspect the Hessian

For 0 < |V₁|/EG ≪ 1, the lower branch has m*ₓ < 0 and m*ᵧ = m > 0: a saddle. The upper branch has both masses positive: a minimum. The quadratic expansion needs |qₓ|/G ≪ |V₁|/(2EG), a narrower region than just |q| ≪ G.

Both normal velocities vanish at qₓ = 0. Reflection and reciprocal periodicity make each smooth band even in qₓ about the zone boundary, so ∂E/∂qₓ = 0. At V₁ = 0 the crossing is unsplit; the sorted branches have a cusp and this nondegenerate argument does not apply.

Below the saddle, hyperbolic branches open along qₓ; above it, they open along qᵧ. At the critical energy they meet along two straight asymptotes. This reconnection is a Lifshitz transition, without requiring broken symmetry.

3(a–c) · Why a step becomes a logarithm

Minimum or maximum: a finite step

For a minimum use Θ(E − Ec); for a maximum use Θ(Ec − E). Rescale the momenta by the square roots of the curvature magnitudes. The contour is a circle: its shrinking circumference cancels the vanishing radial energy gradient, leaving a constant DOS on one side.

Saddle: a logarithm

After rescaling, E − Ec = u² − v². With ξ = u + v and ζ = u − v, the energy is ξζ and du dv = ½ dξ dζ. Integrating the delta function gives an integral proportional to ∫dξ/|ξ|, between an energy-dependent lower bound and a cutoff.

Contour length divided by speed

Near a saddle, long hyperbolic arms approach the crossing while |∇E| becomes small. Their weighted length diverges logarithmically on both sides of Ec. These are the same branches that reconnect in Problem 2.

The prefactors here include two spins and one critical point. Add symmetry-related points to obtain the band’s total singular contribution. X and Y are two inequivalent saddle points on the square-zone torus; ±X are two drawings of the same point.

The cutoff controls the additive regular part of the saddle DOS. The plotted numerical integral uses |√A qₓ|, |√|B| qᵧ| ≤ 1 and replaces δ by η/[π(ε² + η²)]. η is numerical/energy resolution, not automatically temperature. A rounded peak is not a finite clean-limit DOS.
