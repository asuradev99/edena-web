01

Coordinate spaces, and why perspective is a division

OrbitCamera · projectWith · matrix(w/h) · perspective ÷ w

PROJECTION

The library keeps one right-handed world with +Y up. A Visual holds a position, a scale and a rotation; the renderer multiplies those into a model matrix, then the camera multiplies again into clip space, and only at the very end does the GPU divide by w. An orthographic camera writes w = 1, so that division changes nothing: an object's size on screen is the same at every depth. A perspective camera writes w = depth, so the same division makes an object half as wide when it is twice as far away. That is the whole difference, and it is why OrbitCamera.projection defaults to 'orthographic' — it is the more predictable choice, and the one the plots want.

The lab below draws five identical boxes receding along the view axis and measures the width of the nearest and farthest one through the real camera, so the readout cannot drift from the picture. Switch the projection, then pull the camera back: under perspective the near/far ratio is exactly the depth ratio; under orthographic it stays 1.00× however far you go, because distance simply is not in the equation.

Limits. Perspective projection has a near plane and a far plane; geometry behind the eye is clipped, and far defaults to 1000 in the library's units. There is no field-of-view slider on the page, but camera.fovY is public. Labels are DOM overlays projected by the same camera, so a point behind the camera cannot be labelled — the label is placed off-canvas rather than mirrored forward.

02

CPU meshes, GPU batches

functionSurface · parametricSurface · the sample budget · per-vertex colours

GEOMETRY

Every primitive in src/lib/geometry.ts is built on the CPU and returns an immutable Geometry: a flat Float32Array of triangle vertices, optionally with a parallel colour array. functionSurface(f, x, y, resolution) evaluates f on an nu × nv grid, writes two triangles per quad, and hands the result to the renderer. The renderer uploads each distinct Geometry once and then batches every Visual that shares it into one instanced draw, which is why a hundred copies of a sphere cost one buffer and one draw call.

That has a concrete cost model, and the lab fits a curve at increasing resolution while timing the build. Both counters matter: an nu × nv surface evaluates (nu+1)(nv+1) samples but the library's guard counts quads (nu·nv), so a square grid is legal to exactly 500×500. The isosurface extractor spends the same 250 000 budget against a different counter — its sample lattice is (nx+1)(ny+1)(nz+1), so a cubic field tops out at 61³. The probe button below asks for one step too many and prints the library's own refusal.

Limits. Mesh generation is single-threaded and synchronous on the main thread, so a large rebuild is a visible stall — the timing shown is real, not amortised. Geometry is treated as immutable; changing topology means allocating a new one, which is exactly what a slider does here. And the budget is a hard refusal, not a soft downgrade: exceed it and the call throws rather than silently decimating.

03

Colour is data, and the range is a choice

colorMappedSurface · viridis · plasma · ramp · range clamp

COLOUR

Geometry.colors is optional. When it is present the renderer switches to its second pipeline and multiplies the per-vertex colour by the Visual's own colour, so passing white shows the map at full strength and any other tint darkens it. colorMappedSurface(f, x, y, colorOf, resolution, range) builds such a Geometry directly: it samples f, normalises each value into [0, 1], and calls colorOf once per vertex.

The subtle part is the range. With no range the surface uses its own sampled minimum and maximum, which is convenient and quietly dishonest: the picture changes when the data does. Pass an explicit range and every value outside it is clamped to the palette's first or last colour — two different values become indistinguishable. The lab prints the sampled min and max beside the chosen range and the percentage of samples pinned to an end, so you can see exactly what the clamp is hiding.

Limits. viridis and plasma here are nine measured stops with linear interpolation between them, an approximation of the real perceptually-uniform maps rather than a lookup table. ramp requires strictly increasing stops and clamps outside its domain. Colour is linear RGB with no gamma or wide-gamut handling; the renderer blends in the canvas' preferred format and leaves colour management to the browser.

04

Integration, and the order you can measure

Euler vs RK4 · exact e-t · observed order · float64 round-off

NUMERICS

An animation is a differential equation solved one frame at a time. The library's stepParticles is semi-implicit Euler — the cheapest useful scheme — and the GPU particle core uses the same method. The lab compares Euler with classical fourth-order Runge–Kutta on y′ = −y, y(0) = 1, whose exact solution e−t can be drawn beside them.

The honest measure is not one error but how the error falls when the step is halved. Halve the step and Euler's error roughly halves; RK4's falls by about sixteen. The page reports that ratio as the observed order, log₂(e(dt) / e(dt/2)), computed from two real runs. Expect about 1.0 and 4.0 — matching the textbook, and a useful way to catch a bug in an integrator that only looks right at one step size.

Limits. At dt = 0.00625 over two seconds the RK4 error is near 1e-11 and the estimate gets noisy as float64 round-off takes over; the order is an estimate from two runs, not a proof. The global error is also not the picture: a transient can be visually indistinguishable at a step size that is quantitatively wrong. And RK4 costs four evaluations per step, so it is not free accuracy — the right choice depends on the frame budget.

05

The simulation seam, and where the GPU takes over

createParticleState · stepParticles · semi-implicit Euler · GpuParticleSimulation

COMPUTE

src/lib/simulation.ts is deliberately renderer-agnostic: createParticleState makes plain typed arrays, stepParticles advances them with a callback that returns an acceleration, and nothing there knows WebGPU exists. A page drives it and feeds the result into Visuals. src/lib/particles.ts is the GPU-resident counterpart — ping-pong storage buffers, one compute dispatch per step, rendering instanced straight from the buffer — and it uses the same semi-implicit Euler update, so moving to the GPU changes throughput, not accuracy.

This lab runs the CPU seam on a three-dimensional harmonic well, where total energy should be constant. Symplectic Euler does not conserve that energy exactly; its error is bounded and oscillates rather than growing linearly, which is why it is the right cheap scheme for a game loop. The readout shows the measured step rate and the energy drift, and a substep selector shows the obvious trade: more substeps per frame means less drift and proportionally more work.

Limits. The measured step rate is CPU-side and single-threaded, and it includes the loop overhead of calling stepParticles once per substep. The GPU path is far faster for large counts — the project measured tens of thousands of bodies at a fraction of a millisecond per step — but it has fixed overheads, needs an adapter with compute support, and renders without a depth attachment. Nothing here simulates collisions or long-range forces; those are explicit non-goals of the baseline.

06

Symmetry, orbits, and the tolerance that decides

latticeSites · CUBIC_OPERATIONS · siteMapping · symmetryOrbits · mapsOntoSelf

SYMMETRY

A symmetry operation is a map, so the questions worth asking are mechanical: where does each atom go, which atoms stay put, and which trade places? siteMapping(structure, operation) answers the first (one target index per site), symmetryOrbits groups the sites that permute among themselves, and mapsOntoSelf asks the species-aware whole-structure question. The cubic cell's point group has 48 operations, but the same C₄ about z fixes four atoms in a face-centred lattice and only three in a perovskite — the operation is identical; the basis is not.

All of this happens in fractional coordinates, and real crystals are not exact. Every test therefore carries a tolerance, here 1e-4. The strain slider displaces one atom; while the displacement is below tolerance the structure still counts as symmetric, and once it crosses the report flips to onto-self: no. That flip is the honest centre of this chapter: "symmetric" is a statement about a tolerance, not an absolute.

Limits. The viewer's operations are the cell's own point group computed from its metric — 48 for a cubic cell, fewer for lower symmetry. It does not check translations, so screw and glide operations are outside this panel even though the parser preserves them; and 1e-4 is a chosen default, not a physical constant. Change the tolerance and you change the verdict.

07

The transform stack: local moves, composed

Visual.matrix · Group.flatten · multiply · applyMatrix

SCENE GRAPH

A node does not store a world position. It stores a local position, scale and rotation, and Visual.matrix turns those into one 4×4. Group.flatten() then walks the tree multiplying each child's matrix onto its parent's, so a node's world matrix is the product of every ancestor's. That is why moving an arm moves the hand without the hand knowing anything about the shoulder.

The lab is three nested groups, each drawn in its parent's frame starting at the origin. Drag a joint and the whole chain downstream swings; the readout prints the wrist's world position read back out of the graph, and the matrix beside it is the top-left 3×3 the renderer actually used. The animation drives the wrist joint until you touch a slider, so you can watch composition and then take over.

Limits. Visual.rotation is a single y-rotation; a full pose needs the optional Euler orientation, which is applied x→y→z inside the node's frame before the y-rotation. There is no inverse lookup, no constraints and no skinning — this is a transform chain, not a skeleton.

08

Interpolation, easing, and why time is absolute

Timeline · tween · lerp · smooth · cues fire on every seek

MOTION

lerp(a, b, t) is the straight line; smooth(t) = t²(3−2t) is the ease that starts and ends at zero speed; tween(time, start, duration, from, to) is a delayed, eased lerp. Easing changes when a value arrives, never where it starts or ends — which is why the three markers below always share their first and last positions.

The important idea is that a Timeline cue is a function of absolute time. Seek to 2.5 s and every cue is evaluated at 2.5 s, from scratch. Playing is just seeking with an accumulating clock, so scrubbing, pausing, replaying and jumping all give the same picture — and a cue must therefore set absolute state rather than add a delta.

Limits. A cue outside the timeline's duration is rejected rather than clipped; times are clamped into [0, duration]. Easing here is closed-form, so there is no way to author a spring or a bezier curve — a page that wants one writes its own ease. And smooth is C¹, not C²: its acceleration jumps at the ends.

09

A curve is a sampled path, and a path is a tube

functionCurve · polyline · MAX_LINE_POINTS · chunking

CURVES

There are no lines in the renderer, only triangles, so polyline(points, width, sides) sweeps a ring along the path and joins consecutive rings with quads. A curve is therefore two decisions: how finely you sample it, and how round the tube is. functionCurve(f, domain, samples, width) does the sampling and hands the points to polyline at the default four sides, which costs 8 triangles per segment.

Two budgets meet here. The sampler refuses more than 100 000 points, and the tube has its own point cap; when a long run would hit the cap the library chunks it and repeats the shared joint, so the seam is a continuous tube rather than a gap. The probe button asks for one sample past the cap and prints the refusal, because the honest way to show a guard is to trip it.

Limits. The tube width is world-space, so a curve keeps its thickness when you zoom; a screen-space line would be crisper at high zoom and is not implemented. Sampling is uniform in x, so a curve with a sharp feature between two samples is missed exactly there, and the triangle count grows linearly with the sample count — this is a quality/size dial, not an adaptive mesh.

10

Surfaces, quads, and the winding that makes a volume positive

parametricSurface · functionSurface · outward winding · divergence theorem

SURFACES

A parametric surface is two nested loops: evaluate the map on an nu × nv grid, then emit two triangles per quad. Nothing culls or lights, so the order of those three vertices is not cosmetic — it fixes the surface normal. The library winds every closed solid so that du × dv points outward, which has a checkable consequence: the signed volume Σ a·(b×c)/6 comes out positive.

The lab is a torus with that sum printed beside its exact volume 2π²Rr². Turn the resolution up and the estimate converges to the closed form; the sign never changes, which is the whole point. The same winding rule is what lets the consistency test pin every primitive in the module at once.

Limits. The grid is uniform, so a surface that curves sharply in one corner needs resolution everywhere. The guard counts quads — a square grid is legal to exactly 500×500 — and it throws rather than decimating. Non-finite samples are skipped, which can leave a hole rather than a repair.

11

One level set, cut out by marching tetrahedra

isosurface(f, bounds, iso, resolution) · sample budget · non-finite cells

IMPLICIT

An implicit surface is the set {p : f(p) = level}. To draw it, isosurface samples f on a lattice, splits every cell into six tetrahedra, and walks the sixteen inside/outside patterns to emit the triangles where the level crosses an edge — the marching-tetrahedra analogue of marching cubes, with no ambiguous cases. The edges are interpolated linearly, so the surface is exact at the samples and approximate between them.

The cost model is a lattice: an n³ grid evaluates (n+1)³ samples, and the budget is 250 000 samples — not quads, which is the opposite of the surface builders. A cubic field therefore tops out at 61³. The slider melts the level through two metaball singularities, and the readout counts the triangles the extractor actually produced.

Limits. Single-threaded and synchronous: a large rebuild is a visible stall. The sample budget is a hard refusal. Sampling a singularity can produce a non-finite value, and the library suppresses every cell that touches one rather than drawing nonsense — so a pole in the field becomes a hole, deliberately.

12

Field lines: RK4 with arc-length steps

VectorField · streamline · streamlines · sphereSeeds · stagnation points

VECTOR FIELDS

A streamline solves dx/ds = v̂(x) — the field's direction, not its magnitude — so step is an arc length and lines do not bunch up where the field is strong. Each step is a classic four-stage Runge–Kutta evaluation with the intermediate points renormalised. Seeding a golden-spiral shell of directions (sphereSeeds) spreads lines without clustering, and streamlines drops any trace too short to read.

The lab's field has a stagnation point at the origin, where |v| falls below minStrength and the trace stops. The readout says how many of the seeds survived, and a marker walks the first surviving line to show that equal slider steps cover equal distance.

Limits. A trapped seed is discarded, not drawn as a dot, so a field with many stagnation points looks sparser than it is. The integration has no adaptive step and no error estimate: tolerances are a step size you choose, not a bound the code guarantees. Lines are traced independently, so nothing enforces that they avoid each other.

13

Axes, ticks, and choosing round numbers

niceStep · tickValues · tickMath · formatTick · plotFrame

PLOTTING

A chart's gridlines should land on numbers a reader already knows how to divide. niceStep(span, count) picks the smallest of 1, 2, 5 × 10ⁿ that fits the requested number of intervals, tickValues walks multiples of that step inside the range, and tickMath/formatTick print each one — a real minus sign (U+2212, not a hyphen), grouped digits, and an exponent when the value needs one.

Ask for more ticks than the span can hold at a nice step and you get the same step, not a denser grid: the count is a request, and the round number wins. The readout prints the ticks it chose so the rule is visible rather than asserted. plotFrame wraps all of that into geometry — axes, major ticks, exact minor subdivisions, gridlines — plus label anchors that carry plain text and MathML together.

Limits. Minor ticks subdivide the major step by a fixed integer (five by default) and skip the majors themselves. Titles are positioned by a fixed offset from the axis end, so an unusually long tick label can still crowd one — and the frame is geometry, so changing a range means building a new one.

14

Typesetting: builders, operands, and a real minus sign

mathtext.ts · frac · msup · mroot · cases · number() · the operand rule

TYPESETTING

A formula here is a string of MathML elements, built by functions rather than written by hand. The builders exist because MathML has a rule that is easy to break: msup, msub, mfrac, mover and munder take exactly their arguments as children. Hand msup a fragment with two top-level nodes and Chrome does not raise the exponent — it lays a second base beside the first, which is how (x+h)² printed as (x+h)2 for a while. Every builder that takes an operand now groups a multi-root fragment into one mrow first, and a test pins that rule.

Numbers get the same care. number(−12.5, 1) emits a genuine minus sign (U+2212, not a hyphen), groups digits with a thin space in the thousands, and switches to a real msup exponent at the extremes, so a readout lines up with the axes it annotates.

Limits. The builders cover what the pages need — fractions, scripts, roots, matrices, cases, limits, integrals, accents — not the whole of MathML. Nothing sanitises a caller's own fragment, so a page that builds raw MathML is on its own; and the rendering is the browser's, so a face without an OpenType MATH table cannot stretch a delimiter or place a limit.

15
An animated derivation: tokens, beats and marks
Derivation · checkStep · strikeRuns · highlight · bracket · cancel
DERIVATION

      

        
A derivation is a list of lines; a line is a list of tokens, each with its own typeset fragment and a plain-text form; and the explanation is a list of beats, each of which applies marks to tokens by id. Nothing is written twice: the highlighter does not re-typeset a line, it tints the tokens that are already there, and a bracket mark draws its brace from the measured box of the tokens it names — which is why the marks survive a resize.

        
Because the model is data, it can be checked. checkStep refuses a mark that names a token the line does not contain — a typo that would otherwise fail silently — and the button below trips it on purpose. strikeRuns is the other half of the honesty: a cancellation over tokens that are not neighbours cannot be one straight line, so the ids are split into contiguous runs and each run gets its own stroke. A single diagonal through the terms between them was a real bug, and it is pinned by a test.

        
Limits. Marks are per line, so a term that moves between lines cannot be followed by id. The animation is a timer over beats, not a Timeline: it cannot be scrubbed to an arbitrary moment, though seek(index) jumps to a step. And the accessible description is generated from the tokens' plain text, so a token without a text is silent.

16
Picking: a ray through a pixel, and a plane to land on
camera.ray · rayPlane · screenDistance · attachHandles · camera.locked
INTERACTION

      

        
The pointer gives a pixel; the camera turns it into a ray. An orthographic camera gets parallel rays (the origin walks across the camera plane and the direction is constant); a perspective camera gets a pinhole, with one origin at the eye and a direction per pixel. Intersecting that ray with a plane — here the floor, y = 0 — is one dot product away, and the result is the world point the reader is pointing at.

        
Picking a handle is measured in pixels, not world units: screenDistance projects the handle and compares screen positions, so a small object far away is hard to grab and a large one near is easy, which is what the pointer expects. While a handle is held, the camera is locked for the duration of the gesture, so orbiting and dragging never fight over the same movement. The gold ball drags along the floor; the pink echo shows a point that would not be grabbed from the canvas centre.

        
Limits. Picking is nearest-handle-wins against a pixel radius, with no occlusion: a handle hidden behind geometry can still be grabbed. Ray–plane is the only intersection implemented — no triangles, no boxes — so a page that needs surface picking must test its own geometry. And the plane is fixed at grab time, so a handle cannot switch planes mid-drag.

17
Depth, transparency, and the order the renderer chooses
opaque-first · depthWriteEnabled · back-to-front blending · the alpha trap
COMPOSITING

      

        
Depth is a per-pixel competition. An opaque object writes its depth as it draws, so anything later that is farther away loses the test. A translucent object must not write depth — otherwise it would hide what is behind it — so it only tests against the opaque depth already there, and the renderer sorts those objects back to front by their origin before blending them.

        
That rule has a consequence worth knowing. A translucent surface has no depth in the buffer, so it cannot hide itself: two transparent faces that intersect in space will both be drawn, in the order the sort chose, and the picture will show the seam. The comb of fins that appeared in the contrast demo came from exactly this — a surface at alpha .95 took the translucent path — and the fix was to make it opaque. Remove the core below and the shells have nothing to test against, so they read as flat panes rather than a nesting.

        
Limits. Sorting is per object, by origin depth, so a large translucent surface can still be wrong against another that intersects it; there is no depth peeling and no order-independent transparency. Opaque geometry is drawn in scene order, which is fine because depth resolves it; translucent geometry keeps its given order among equals.

18
Instancing: one mesh, N draws, and the bytes that move
one upload per Geometry · 17 floats per instance · only the changed interval
BATCHING

      

        
Geometry is uploaded once per distinct Geometry object, no matter how many Visuals share it. Everything that differs between those visuals — the model matrix and the opacity — travels in an instance buffer: 16 floats for the 4×4 and one for the opacity, so 17 floats, 68 bytes, per object. A batch of a thousand spheres is one vertex buffer, one instance buffer and one draw call.

        
The second half of the contract is what happens per frame. The renderer compares the packed float values against what it last uploaded and uploads only the changed interval, so a still scene transfers nothing and one object moving transfers a handful of bytes. The readout does that arithmetic out loud; the renderer's own upload counts are measured by scripts/check-renderer.mjs, which asserts that an idle frame writes zero bytes.

        
Limits. A batch is keyed by geometry, colour and transparency, so a thousand copies in a thousand different colours is a thousand batches and loses the win — the colour is per batch, not per instance. Losing one instance is a draw count change, not a buffer edit. And the comparison is a full pass over the instance data each frame: cheap for thousands, not for millions.

19
Crystal data: reading a POSCAR, and the scale that is a volume
parsePOSCAR · parsePhonopySymmetry · fractional → cartesian · negative scale
FILE FORMATS

      

        
A POSCAR is a small text file with a scale, three lattice vectors, a line of species and a line of counts, then fractional coordinates. The parser is strict about the parts that matter and tolerant about the parts that do not: blank lines and trailing comments are fine, the Direct/Cartesian switch is honoured, and the species line may be either symbols or numbers.

        
The one rule that surprises people is the scale. A positive scale multiplies every lattice vector. A negative scale is not a length at all: VASP reads its magnitude as a target cell volume and rescales the vectors so the cell encloses that many cubic ångström. A parser that treated it as a multiplier would silently shrink a cell by two orders of magnitude, so the second preset uses one and the readout prints the volume it implies.

        
The companion reader, parsePhonopySymmetry, takes the symmetry block of a phonopy file — rotation matrices and translations — and returns labelled operations, which is what the symmetry chapter consumes. Both parsers are pure text functions with no DOM, so the test suite feeds them the awkward files: Windows line endings, extra blank lines, a species line of bare numbers, and a missing count.

        
Limits. Only the POSCAR/phonopy shapes are understood: no symmetry operations from a CIF, no selective dynamics flags, no per-site labels beyond the species. Fractional coordinates are used as given — the parser does not reduce them into the cell — and a malformed file throws with a message rather than guessing.

20
Element appearance: one table for colour and size
ELEMENTS · appearanceFor · covalent radii · the unknown-symbol fallback
DATA TABLE

      

        
Every element is drawn the same way: a sphere whose radius is a scaled covalent radius and whose colour is the CPK convention — white hydrogen, black carbon, red oxygen, blue nitrogen, yellow sulfur, green chlorine, and a muted palette for the metals so a structure of forty species still reads. The table lives in one place, ELEMENTS, and appearanceFor looks a symbol up, trims and title-cases it, and falls back to a neutral grey for anything it does not know.

        
The colours here are the library's own: chosen for a dark background and pushed slightly apart in hue, rather than the CMK/CPK values a print diagram uses. Radii are in ångström and are consistent with the lattice chapter, so a bond drawn between two spheres meets both surfaces — the panel below prints the fallback so a reader can see that an unknown symbol still draws something.

        
Limits. A static table, not a periodic table: only the symbols it lists are styled, radii are one number per element rather than a radius per bond order, and there is no isotope or charge distinction. Because the renderer is unlit, a sphere in a pale colour reads flatter than in a rendered figure — the same reason every mesh here now carries a wireframe.

21
Area under a curve: the picture is the quadrature rule
areaUnder · the trapezoid sum · error against the closed form · O(1/n²)
QUADRATURE

      

        
A definite integral is a limit of sums, and the strips drawn here are one of those sums: areaUnder(f, [a, b], { baseline, samples }) emits one quad per interval between two samples, with corners at (left, f(left)) and (right, f(right)). That is exactly the trapezoid rule, so the number the panel prints is not an illustration of the picture — it is the same arithmetic the triangles are built from.

        
Because each row of the table also knows its closed form — ∫₀^π sin = 2, ∫₀¹ x² = 1/3, ∫₀¹ √x = 2/3 — the error is a measurement rather than a claim. The trapezoid rule on a smooth function is second order: halve the width and the error falls by about four. Watch the exponent as the strip count doubles: that factor of four is the order of the method.

        
Limits. The rule is trapezoid, not Simpson: there is no higher-order scheme to choose, and a function with a corner or a singularity inside the range converges far more slowly than the table suggests — √x on [0, 1] is included precisely because its endpoint slope is infinite, and its error falls like 1/n^1.5, not 1/n². The strips also follow the samples rather than adapting to them, so a feature between two samples is integrated wrongly with no warning.

The difference quotient, and the limit that defines a derivative
secantSlope · lineThrough · h → 0 · the truncation error of a chord
CALCULUS

      

        
The derivative is a limit of slopes of secants: f′(a) = limh→0 (f(a+h) − f(a))/h, which is exactly what secantSlope(f, a, a+h) computes. The panel draws one fixed tangent — the answer — and a secant whose h you can push down until the two are indistinguishable at the precision a float32 mesh can carry.

        
The error behaves the way Taylor's theorem says it must. The chord slope is the average of the derivative over the interval, so the difference from f′(a) is O(h) for a smooth function: every division of h by ten removes roughly one digit of error, until floating-point cancellation in f(a+h) − f(a) starts to cost digits instead. That is the honest ending to this chapter — the limit exists in analysis, but a computer reaches the noise floor first, and the slider goes there.

        
Limits. Nothing here differentiates symbolically: the derivative is written out by hand in the lab, and there is no automatic differentiation in the library. The slider is logarithmic on purpose, and the last decade is where the round-off appears rather than the mathematics.
